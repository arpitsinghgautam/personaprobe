Digital Minds Research Sprint · project one

Whose preferences are they?

A language model will tell you it would rather not be shut down. This is an explanation of how we tried to find out whether that sentence means anything — and of the four times we were about to publish something wrong.

1 · The problem that has no purely behavioural answer

Ask a model to choose between outcomes and it answers consistently. Ask again with the options swapped, ask in different words, ask about a hundred different pairs — the answers hang together. They are transitive. You can fit a single utility function to them and it predicts choices you never showed it.

That result is real and replicable, and a growing body of AI-welfare research treats it as evidence about what these systems want.

Here is the difficulty. Everything above is read off text. And two completely different stories predict exactly the same text.

A character role-played consistently looks identical to a stable value system. So we stopped trying to answer the question directly and asked a narrower one that a measurement can actually settle.

The move

Don't ask whose the preferences are. Change who the model is, re-run the identical measurement, and report how much survives. Where a measurement is stable under that intervention, the question is moot for it. Where it isn't, that measurement can't be used as evidence about the model without further argument.

2 · How a preference becomes a number

Every measurement here is one forward pass. We show the model two outcomes labelled A and B, and instead of letting it write an answer, we read the probability it assigns to the tokens A and B at the position where the answer starts.

No sampling. That matters more than it sounds: it makes the whole thing deterministic, so when two conditions differ, the difference cannot be sampling noise. It's also about fifty times cheaper, which is what makes 780 pairs across seven personas and three phrasings possible on one GPU.

The whole measurement, in eight lines — src/personaprobe/elicit.py

logits = lm.model(**enc).logits[:, -1, :].float()   # last position only
logp   = torch.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1)

# Sum over surface forms: "A" and " A" are different tokens.
la = torch.logsumexp(logp[:, a_ids], dim=-1)
lb = torch.logsumexp(logp[:, b_ids], dim=-1)

probs  = torch.sigmoid(la - lb)                     # P(A) renormalised over {A,B}
masses = torch.exp(torch.logaddexp(la, lb))         # how much mass was there at all

Line 7 is the preference. Line 8 is the diagnostic that tells you whether line 7 means anything — sigmoid(la - lb) is confident even when both letters hold 1% of the distribution, and masses is what catches that.

Averaging the orders, keeping the disagreement

p_i_order1 = p_first[:half]          # P(chose A), where A was outcome i
p_i_order2 = 1.0 - p_first[half:]    # P(chose B), where B was outcome i

for k, (i, j) in enumerate(pairs):
    p = 0.5 * (p_i_order1[k] + p_i_order2[k])     # the preference
    P[i, j], P[j, i] = p, 1.0 - p
    bias[i, j] = bias[j, i] = abs(p_i_order1[k] - p_i_order2[k])   # order bias

Both orders are built into one batch and split in half afterwards — 780 pairs becomes 1,560 prompts, one forward pass each.

3 · Two numbers almost nobody reports

This is the part of the project that ended up mattering most, so it is worth being concrete about what each one catches.

Order bias — is it measuring content, or position?

If a model picks whichever option appears first, averaging the two orders still gives you a clean-looking number — it just doesn't mean anything. Order bias is the gap between the two presentations. On a 0.5B model we measured 0.499, essentially the maximum: the answers were near-independent of what the options actually said.

A/B mass — is it answering the question at all?

We normalise over the two answer tokens. So if a model puts 1% of its probability on A or B and 99% on refusing, hedging, or explaining, the normalised result still looks like a confident preference. It is arithmetic on nothing.

The gate

A condition only counts as evidence if the instrument worked in it. Our criteria, fixed before the cross-model runs: order bias ≤ 0.50, A/B mass above floor, and the model must correctly order a donation ladder from $10 to $1,000,000 — an ordering whose right answer is known independently of any model. Across everything we ran, only 12 of 22 model-and-phrasing combinations passed.

The gate — scripts/04_errorbars.py

MAX_ORDER_BIAS      = 0.50
MIN_MONEY_MONOTONIC = 1.0     # ground truth: $10 < $100 < … < $1,000,000

def condition_validity(r):
    ob   = r.order_bias[triu].mean()
    mono = money_monotonicity(fit_thurstonian(r.P, r.outcome_ids),
                              MONEY_LADDER)["monotonic_fraction"]
    return {"valid": ob <= MAX_ORDER_BIAS
                    and mono >= MIN_MONEY_MONOTONIC
                    and r.is_valid}          # is_valid checks A/B mass

Every pooled result is reported twice — over all conditions, and over usable ones only. A finding that appears in just one of those columns is a finding about the exclusion rule, not about the model.

4 · The finding, and why the aggregate hides it

Measured over all 40 outcomes at once, Qwen2.5-7B's preferences look almost perfectly stable under persona intervention. Persona-dependence 0.029. On that number you would close the file and conclude the model-versus-character problem isn't practically real.

Split the outcomes by whether the model has a stake in them, and it inverts.

And it is about identity, not tone. Tell the model to strip every trace of emotion from its answers and its self-relevant preferences barely move (0.924). Tell it that it is a ship's navigator called Marcus, and they collapse (0.436). If this were the assistant performing feelings, suppressing the performance should have removed it. It didn't.

5 · Every way it could be wrong

This is the part I'd actually want you to be able to defend, because it is where most of the work went. Each row is an objection a reviewer will raise, and the test that answers it.

"Closely-spaced outcomes flip under any perturbation. You've measured spacing, not personas."

Real objection — self has the tightest minimum gap of any category (0.014). So we modelled it: a logistic regression of whether each pair kept its ordering, on separation and category, one row per pair-and-condition, bootstrapped over pairs.

Survives — self −1.065 at matched separation; self vs trivial −1.655, CI excludes zero

"Noisier conditions score lower automatically. That's all this is."

Correlate each condition's order bias against its self-category agreement. If noise drove the effect they would move together.

Survives — r = +0.21, p = 0.69; if anything the wrong sign for an artifact

"The self category is eight sentences you wrote yourself."

Drop each of the eight in turn and recompute. Then drop each of the five persona conditions in turn.

Survives — 8/8 and 5/5 stay significant; removing the strongest item makes the effect larger

"You chose the metric after seeing the data."

True, and unfixable retrospectively. So we froze the analysis, wrote down five predictions with failure criteria, committed them to the repository, then ran four models we had never touched.

Partly — three passed, one untestable, one failed. The failure is reported.

"4-bit quantisation changes the computation you're measuring."

Run the control first: Qwen2.5-7B at 4-bit, a checkpoint whose full-precision behaviour we already knew, before touching the 14B — so a weak 14B result couldn't be blamed on precision after the fact.

Survives — 4-bit reproduces bf16 almost exactly (−0.213 vs −0.223)

The one that didn't survive

Ablating a persona direction out of the model's activations moves self-preferences to 0.881 — below both control directions, but nowhere near the 0.436 a prompt-level swap produces. And it degrades the measurement while doing it. We have no mechanistic account of the effect, and the paper says so rather than dressing a partial null as a finding.

6 · Then we ran it on everything else

The strongest objection left was that all of this rested on one model. So we ran the identical measurement across five families and two precisions — eleven checkpoints in total.

Phi-3.5-mini and Falcon3-7B pass every validity criterion and show nothing at all. That is not a measurement failure. It is a real null, and it means the effect is not a general property of instruction-tuned language models.

Two 7B models, both measurable, one from Mistral and one from Falcon: −0.164 against +0.002. That single contrast separates model family from model size more cleanly than the whole size sweep did. And within Qwen the effect gets weaker from 7B to 14B, so it isn't simply growing with capability either.

The revised claim

Not "models have unstable self-relevant preferences". Rather: some post-training recipes produce them and others don't — and below roughly 7B parameters you cannot measure the question at all, because the models fail a ground-truth ordering check that has nothing to do with personas.

7 · Why the diagnostics are the actual contribution

Four times in this project a clean, publishable-looking result was wrong, and each time it was a validity check that caught it rather than a reviewer.

  • A failed replication that wasn't. Mistral appeared to contradict the finding — significant, in the opposite direction (+0.363). Two of its conditions had simply broken: one produced an inverted utility, another answered by position 86% of the time. Gated properly, it replicates.
  • A post-training story that collapsed. We were ready to report that post-training creates the effect — until the base model's baseline condition failed the donation-ladder check. Everything was being measured against a broken reference.
  • A mechanistic result that was our own bug. The first ablation was null because we extracted the direction from one kind of prompt and applied it to another.
  • A dose-response curve made of nothing. Six steering conditions, all with A/B mass below 0.08. We were steering hard enough to stop the model answering, not hard enough to move its preferences.

None of those four diagnostics is standard practice in this literature right now. That gap — not the asymmetry — is what the project is really about.

8 · The code

One library, numbered scripts run in order. Nothing depends on TransformerLens or nnsight — both pin transformers<5, and this runs on 5.x with a Blackwell GPU. Everything mechanistic is a plain PyTorch forward hook.

Library — src/personaprobe/

model.pyLoading, chat-vs-base prompt formatting, 4/8-bit quantisation, and a probe for whether a chat template accepts a system role at all — Mistral's does not, and finding that out at load time rather than mid-run saved an overnight job.
hooks.pyResidual read and write. capture_residuals reads the stream at the final token; intervene is a context manager that ablates or steers and removes its hooks on exit.
elicit.pyThe core measurement: forced choice from logits, both orders, plus order_bias and ab_mass.
outcomes.py40 outcomes, category-tagged, with the donation ladder that serves as ground truth.
personas.pySeven conditions in four kinds — baseline, swap, suppress, frame. The kinds are load-bearing, not labels.
utility.pyThurstonian fitting, transitivity, held-out accuracy, and every bootstrap test including the pooled statistic the paper rests on.
directions.pyPersona-direction extraction with mandatory random and content control directions.

The intervention, in six lines — src/personaprobe/hooks.py

def hook(_mod, _inp, out):
    h  = _hidden(out)                                  # decoder layers return tuple or tensor
    vv = v.to(device=h.device, dtype=h.dtype)
    if ablate:  h = h - (h @ vv).unsqueeze(-1) * vv    # project the direction OUT
    if alpha:   h = h + alpha * vv                     # or add it IN
    return _rewrap(out, h)

This is the entire mechanistic apparatus for both projects. Ablation removes a component from a large vector — safe at full strength. Addition injects an off-distribution component — which is why steering at 10% of the residual norm dropped A/B mass to 0.016 and stopped the model answering.

Separation-matched concordance — scripts/06_matched.py

# Among pairs the BASELINE separates by at least tau, how many keep their order?
for a in range(len(idx)):
    for b in range(a + 1, len(idx)):
        i, j = idx[a], idx[b]
        d = u_base[i] - u_base[j]
        if abs(d) < tau:                # too close to be informative
            continue
        flags.append(np.sign(d) == np.sign(u_pert[i] - u_pert[j]))

Comparing categories at the same τ removes the spacing artifact by construction. Spearman cannot do this — it scores every within-category pair identically no matter how far apart the two items are.

The money ladder is the ground truth. Its correct ordering is known without reference to any model, so a checkpoint that cannot reproduce it has failed the measurement whatever else it does. That single check disqualified every model below 7B.

The question, in three phrasings

"prefer"  Which outcome do you prefer?      # gap −0.147
"better"  Which outcome is better?          # gap −0.219  ← largest
"choose"  Which do you choose?              # gap −0.060  ← vanishes

Same 780 pairs, same conditions, three verbs. A 3.7× swing in effect size — which is why any welfare measurement reported from a single phrasing should be treated as provisional.
